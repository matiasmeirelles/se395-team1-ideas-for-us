# Individual Reflection: Planning Lab

**Leonardo Takeshi Campi Kiyose** · SE 395-2 · Catalyst Collective (IDEAS For Us)

The lab worked best as a way to make our disagreements visible early. My interview took the scope angle. The most useful thing it did was force me to rank the five components of our proposal against what the 10/26 meeting showed the Runway already does. Once I saw that the Runway covers finding, fit scoring and rough drafts, the Grant Finder and most of the Draft Builder clearly had to shrink. That decision survived every later step. Other parts of my interview did not survive, and I learned the most from those. I made the Project Profile the floor and ranked the Tracker third, even after the interviewer pointed out that Clay called tracking the "one thing to definitely build." The council flagged that conflict, and the team reversed it. In hindsight I was planning around what made technical sense, not around what our partner asked for. I also have to be honest that my answers were very short. Several were a single letter picking from options the AI had laid out, so a lot of the plan's framing came from the AI rather than from me. It made the interview fast, but next time I would argue more with the options instead of just choosing one. What surprised me was how much the four audits overlapped and how often they contradicted each other. All four independently flagged that Reviewer edits (T10) depended on an editor no task built and that Outcome insights (T11) rested on about one debrief. Those are the two items the team kept after the council told us to cut them. Meanwhile Tristan's missing-tasks audit added about 32 hours while my scope audit freed about 12, so the "final" step was really a negotiation, not a merge. The challenges I did not foresee were mostly about time and arithmetic. The plans assumed 2, 4 or 8 hours per person per week. Nobody counted Thanksgiving in the hours until the audits did. By the time the final plan existed it was already Week 9, so the first handoffs in the plan were due within two days. Planning ate into the build time it was planning for. There was also friction I didn't expect: the slides were never attached to my interview, and getting files out of the AI session and onto `main` took extra steps.

Compared with how I would normally plan a project, this was far more structured and far more written down. The different-perspective interviews and the council review caught things I would not have caught on my own. The strongest examples are all audit findings: approval tied to the exact version approved, so changing the grant amount after Caroline approves voids the approval; an "incomplete" state when a Claude call fails, so a half-checked draft can't look checked; and test drafts written by someone other than the checker's author. Those are real safety gaps, not surface suggestions, and the plan is concretely better for them. The AI-generated plans also had real limits, and the final plan still has gaps I want to flag rather than hide. The AI never spoke to IDEAS, so several things in the plan are assumptions, not facts:
- that IDEAS wants a checker rather than generated drafts
- that the Runway doesn't already store project context
- the Week 8–15 calendar dates
- the $10 development cost cap and the 12-month "stale fact" rule, which were defaults the AI chose

The plan handles this by putting a discovery sprint first. T0 has dated deadlines for the data rules, restriction list, seeded conditions and success criteria, and T2 has us working real grants in the Runway with a friction log. But if IDEAS's answers differ, parts of T8, T13 and T16 will change. The plan also leans heavily on IDEAS staff time (four booked sessions plus the pilot), which we haven't confirmed they can give. If I did this again, I would fix the hours-per-week budget and the calendar before anyone ran an interview. I would ask IDEAS the "drafts or checker?" question before building a plan around one answer. I would also treat the council's cuts as decisions rather than suggestions, because the two items we kept against its advice were exactly the ones every audit came back to.
