# IDEAS for Us: Opportunity Analysis

## Where could AI genuinely help?

Based on the pain points and stakeholder needs, IDEAS for Us is an organization that is already very advanced with AI. From the partner visit, I think the most efficient path is to build them an automated workflow for grants. This would save them time in that area, and as the team becomes more comfortable and better able to feed in inputs, their AI resources can move on to bigger areas.

**Evidence from the visit:** Clayton said his team has trained language models to write like him since 2019, moving to Claude after their own models were outperformed. They improved results by giving Claude five of his past grants as examples and feeding his edits back, which has run for over a year and increased the number of grants they get out quickly. He sorts AI work into utilitarian tasks (receipts, data entry, accounting) and nuanced human work, and he named pain points such as time-consuming grant writing and a day lost on hold trying to recover a USDA portal password. For "bigger areas," he proposed AI analysis of town hall minutes and commissioner voting records, which he said would turn about 40 hours of reading into an afternoon.

## Which opportunities are realistic for their size and budget?

Given the size and scope of the organization, these internal opportunities can be cost-effective. Costs become more constraining, however, when intensive parts of individual projects overlap across multiple projects, which can raise the price.

**Evidence from the visit:** Clayton described low-cost uses, such as scanning receipts with a phone and having Claude turn them into a spreadsheet, and said accounting for a few million dollars is a light load for AI systems. The complexity you describe shows up in their scale: offices in Orlando, St. Petersburg, Miami, Washington D.C., and San Francisco, work in many countries, and hiring that follows each new grant. Budget limits are real, since Claude Corps moved to Cohort 2 because of timing with the state budget. *Not stated in the visit:* any cost figures, so the claim about overlapping project costs is an inference.

## What are the risks of each opportunity?

For the most part, data is always an issue. The data they will be looking at, filling in, and sending is sensitive. Cost could also hurt as they expand, along with the number of projects they take on.

**Evidence from the visit:** Clayton said they are "hiring for all kinds of things all the time" based on the grants they receive, so winning more grants adds projects and staff, which supports the cost risk. *Not stated in the visit:* data sensitivity or privacy, so that risk is currently an assumption (see the Data questions below).

## Top 3 opportunities, ranked by impact and feasibility

### 1. Grant search and submissions

Grant search and submission is the strongest opportunity because it is website-based and has clear parameters, which makes it easier for them to automate and to get better results. It also does this without taking time away from their team.

**Evidence from the visit:** Clayton said grant applications follow a set format, with character counts and questions in boxes, which is why the team knew AI could handle them. In the EPA experiment, he tested how far they could get using AI entirely and reached the top 10 out of thousands of applicants, including large national organizations. He also said their normal practice is a rough AI draft that humans edit, not AI output sent straight off. *Not stated in the visit:* grant search or website-based tools, so that part is an inference.

### 2. Data and reporting

Data and reporting would help them achieve greater impact, but it would not be cost-effective. Gathering the data and making sure it is accurate and actionable would take a lot of work.

**Evidence from the visit:** The biochar project in Hardee County (turning dead citrus trees into biochar on farms) will involve what Clayton called a tremendous amount of information to gather and curate, along with building the case for more funding. He asked students to help with that work. He also said designing metrics and theory of change is where human judgment sets the organization apart. *Not stated in the visit:* cost figures, so the "not cost-effective" conclusion is an inference.

### 3. Automating project steps

Because of the scale of the operation, the different customers, and how closely they work with them, using AI to automate project steps would be difficult. In some cases it would not be allowed because of their customers' preferences.

**Evidence from the visit:** Clayton said AI should free the team for in-person work such as visiting communities, understanding local context, and telling human stories. He called AI a team member at best and an adversary if it erodes the purpose of the work. The scale shows in the five offices, work across many countries, and programs like Fleet Farming. *Not stated in the visit:* customer preferences about automation. The brands he named (AT&T, GM, Sprouts) came up only as grant contexts, so that part is an inference.
